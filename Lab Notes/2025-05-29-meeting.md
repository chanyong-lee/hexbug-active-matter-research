## 2. Buoyancy Discussion

We discussed how to make the Hexbug close to neutrally buoyant in fluid.

To understand buoyancy, we need to know two main quantities:

- the density of the fluid
- the displaced volume of the object

For neutral buoyancy:

```text
Buoyant force = Weight
```

or equivalently:

```text
fluid density × displaced volume = total mass of the object
```

This means that we cannot calculate buoyancy only from the mass of the Hexbug. We also need to measure how much fluid the Hexbug displaces when submerged.

---

## 3. Mineral Oil Density Measurement

We measured the density of the mineral oil using a graduated cylinder and electronic balance.

### Goal

The goal was to determine the density of the mineral oil that will be used in the Hexbug active matter and buoyancy experiments. This value will later be used to estimate the buoyant force acting on the Hexbug and to calculate the amount of additional flotation material needed for neutral buoyancy.

### Method

We measured the mass of mineral oil at three different volumes:

- 20.0 mL
- 30.0 mL
- 40.0 mL

For each trial:

```text
Mass of oil = mass of cylinder with oil - mass of empty cylinder
```

Then:

```text
Density = mass of oil / volume of oil
```

### Data

| Trial | Cylinder Mass (g) | Volume of Oil (mL) | Cylinder + Oil Mass (g) | Oil Mass (g) | Density (g/mL) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 54.21 | 20.0 | 71.02 | 16.81 | 0.8405 |
| 2 | 54.21 | 30.0 | 79.53 | 25.32 | 0.8440 |
| 3 | 54.21 | 40.0 | 88.04 | 33.83 | 0.8458 |

### Result

```text
Average density of mineral oil = 0.8434 g/mL
```

This measured value is close to the expected density range for mineral oil, so we can use it as our working value for buoyancy calculations.

### Sources of Uncertainty

Possible sources of uncertainty include:

- air bubbles in the mineral oil
- oil sticking to the inside wall of the graduated cylinder
- difficulty reading the meniscus exactly
- electronic balance uncertainty
- small errors in measuring volume

Even with these uncertainties, the three trials produced similar density values, so the result seems reliable enough for our next buoyancy calculations.

## 4. Measuring the Hexbug Volume

We also discussed how to measure the volume of the Hexbug Nano. We have not measured the volume yet.

This is important because buoyancy depends on displaced volume, not just mass. The Hexbug has an irregular shape with a curved shell, legs, gaps, and possible trapped air spaces. Therefore, using its external dimensions alone would only give a rough bounding-box estimate and would not accurately represent its true displaced volume.

### Possible Methods

#### Method 1: Graduated Cylinder Displacement

1. Add water to a graduated cylinder.
2. Record the initial volume.
3. Fully submerge the Hexbug using a thin wire or string.
4. Remove air bubbles if possible.
5. Record the final volume.
6. Calculate:

```text
Hexbug volume = final volume - initial volume
```

#### Method 2: Overflow Method

1. Fill a small container completely with water.
2. Place another container underneath to catch overflow.
3. Slowly submerge the Hexbug.
4. Collect the overflow water.
5. Measure the volume of the overflow water.

The overflow volume equals the displaced volume of the Hexbug.

#### Method 3: Scale-Based Archimedes Method

1. Put a cup of water on a scale.
2. Tare the scale to zero.
3. Suspend the Hexbug fully underwater using a thin string or wire.
4. Make sure the Hexbug does not touch the bottom or sides.
5. Record the increase in scale reading.

For water:

```text
displaced volume in cm^3 ≈ scale increase in grams
```

This method may be more precise if the scale is reliable.

### Important Note

We should eventually measure the volume of the modified or waterproofed Hexbug, not only the bare Hexbug. The waterproofing material, tape, coating, or added flotation material will change the actual displaced volume of the final experimental object.

## 6. Updated Project Direction

The project now has two connected tracks.

### Track 1: Hexbug Active Matter Experiments

The Hexbug track will focus on measuring and analyzing motion.

Main measurements:

- speed
- trajectory
- turning radius
- angular velocity
- clockwise or counterclockwise motion
- behavior in different fluids
- behavior under different buoyancy conditions

This connects directly to the *Playing with Active Matter* paper, where Hexbugs are used as macroscopic active particles. Our work can be framed as an extension of that idea, especially by testing different fluids and buoyancy conditions.

### Track 2: Self-Propelled Robot Design

The second track is to design and eventually build a custom self-propelled robot.

Main design components:

- motor
- power source
- microsensor / data logger
- temperature sensor
- waterproof casing
- removable access door
- optional 3D-printed corkscrew tail inspired by bacterial flagella

This robot will be designed in Fusion 360 and may be 3D printed. The goal is to create a small self-propelled system that can be tested in non-Newtonian fluids.

If possible, the robot will use a corkscrew-like tail to explore bacteria-inspired propulsion.

## 7. Self-Propelled Robot Design Discussion

We discussed the early design of the self-propelled robot.

The robot should include:

- a motor to generate motion
- a power source to run the motor and sensor
- a microsensor or data logger to store experimental data
- a temperature sensor probe extending outside the body
- a waterproof internal compartment
- an access door or hatch so the microsensor can be removed later
- a possible 3D-printed corkscrew tail for bacteria-inspired locomotion

The sensor may be able to store data internally. After the experiment, we can remove the sensor and connect it to a laptop or computer to extract the data.

Therefore, the casing must be able to open and close, or at least have a removable door that allows access to the microsensor.

### Design Requirements

The self-propelled robot should be:

- compact
- waterproof
- easy to open for data retrieval
- balanced or symmetric if possible
- large enough to contain electronics
- small enough to behave like a useful experimental robot
- modular enough to test different tails or propulsion ideas

## 9. Waterproofing Discussion

We also talked about waterproofing. This will likely require trial and error.

Things that need to be waterproofed:

- motor
- power source
- microsensor
- internal wiring
- sensor connector or port
- access door/hatch

Possible waterproofing challenges:

- water or oil leaking through the access door
- sensor cable pass-through leaking
- difficulty sealing the motor shaft or tail connection
- difficulty keeping the microsensor removable while still sealing the body
- added waterproofing material changing buoyancy and weight distribution

Professor Acres emphasized that we do not need to make the design perfect from the beginning. We should build a simple version first, test it, see what fails, and improve it step by step.
